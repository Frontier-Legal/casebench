A report is evidence. Like any evidence, it supports some statements and not others. These rules keep what you say within what it proves.

## Always give four things

1. **What was tested**: the kind of question and the practice area. "40 employment questions written by our lawyers."
2. **Where the answer key came from**: court decisions or your lawyers, and whether a lawyer approved it. The exported report lists who approved it.
3. **The figure with its range**: "unsupported claims in 14% of answers, between 4% and 31%."
4. **What changed**, when comparing: "against 43% for the previous version."

## Sentences a report supports

- "In 40 approved employment questions, 2 answers cited a decision that does not exist in our corpus."
- "Our lawyers preferred the new version in 12 of 20 comparisons and the old in 3; the difference is significant."
- "The two versions cannot be told apart on these 7 questions."

## Sentences it does not support

| Do not say | Why |
| --- | --- |
| "It is 86% accurate." | There is no single accuracy figure, and the range is missing. |
| "Version B is better." | Not when the comparison is marked not significant. |
| "It never fabricates citations." | Only that none were found in these answers, against this corpus. |
| "Lawyers approved the results." | Lawyers approved the answer key and reviewed answers. Say which, and say which rows are unreviewed. |

## When a buyer asks for evidence

The strongest evidence is a result they can repeat. Casebench is open source and runs on their own sources: give them the suite's name and its fingerprint from the report, and they can test the same system on the same cases.
